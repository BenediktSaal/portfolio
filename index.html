Agent workflow

Service ticket triage

Customer service inboxes are mostly routine with a few cases that are anything but. This agent reads every incoming message, decides whether it can be answered, and writes the reply — so the team confirms and sends instead of composing from scratch. Anything sensitive is taken out of the automated path by a fixed rule and handed to a person, together with a note on what to check first. Shown on twelve synthetic requests from an insurer's inbox.

Where this came from

Working alongside service teams, the pattern was always the same: the sorting effort repeats hundreds of times a day, while the cases that genuinely need judgement are the rare ones.

Read the run report One complete run · 12 tickets · every decision listed
Situation
One unsorted inbox, worked in the order things arrived
What it does
Reads each message, sorts it, writes the reply or the briefing for a person
Effect
3 of 12 came back ready to send, 8 went to a case handler, 1 was spam
01

The problem

  • Everything lands in one inbox as free text, so the priority of a request only becomes visible once somebody has read the whole thing
  • Experienced case handlers spend the day on questions a standard answer would cover
  • The cases that need a fast reaction — suspected fraud, a changed bank account, a cancellation — are found late, often only after the customer writes a second time
  • How to sort is nowhere written down; it sits with the experienced staff, so the quality depends on who is on shift
02

How it works

One message in, one finished proposal out. Nothing is ever sent to a customer — every output waits for a person to confirm it.

In
A free-text message
Whatever the customer wrote. No form, no category, no priority.
Step 1
Sorted into one of four
Answer it, ask for what is missing, hand it to a person, or set it aside as spam.
Step 2
Checked against fixed rules
Fraud, payment changes, cancellations, refund disputes, legal threats and injury always go to a person — whatever the agent concluded.
Out
A reply or a briefing
A draft ready to send, a question back to the customer, or what the case handler should check first.
03

What it changes

Measured on a full run: three of twelve tickets came back finished, two answers and one question back to the customer. Everything contractual or financial the agent passed to a person on its own — so the realistic saving is about a quarter of the volume, not half. The rule had to step in once, on the ticket that said nothing beyond “something isn't right”.

Before
·Read, sort and compose for every single request
·Escalation depends on experience and on how full the queue is
·No data on how the volume actually splits
After
→Confirm and send for the routine share
→Sensitive cases leave the automated path by rule, with a briefing attached
→Every decision logged, so the next automation step can be argued with numbers

Limitation. The demo works on files. In a live setting the same workflow would sit on the ticket system or the CRM and write its proposal back as an internal note — the division of labour stays the same: the agent proposes, the rules decide, a person sends.

Twelve synthetic insurance tickets, no real customer data.

Performance dialogue

Operations cockpit

The board for a weekly performance dialogue, the format used on the production floor and in the management review, here with six KPIs of an airline catering unit. It is filled from the figures that arrive by mail, marks whether a deviation is a single bad week or a repeated miss, and carries every agreed measure — owner, due date, status, effect — until it is closed.

Where this came from

Preparing the weekly service and after-sales reporting, the preparation itself was the inefficient part — collecting and retyping figures that were already written down somewhere.

Open the cockpit Synthetic data · 13 weeks · 6 KPIs · opens in this browser
Situation
Figures arrive as prose in mails and are retyped by hand every week
What it does
Reads the mails, checks the values, sorts the deviations and tracks the measures agreed on them
Effect
The review starts with the discussion instead of the status
01

The problem

  • The figures come in as sentences in mails from shift leads, quality and controlling — roughly an hour a week goes into transferring them into the board
  • The meeting spends its first half establishing what the numbers did, in slide order rather than by importance
  • A one-off outlier and a two-month drift get the same airtime, although one needs a cause and the other a decision
  • Agreed measures live in the minutes and come back only if somebody remembers them
02

How it works

Step 1
Collect
A scheduled run reads the KPI mails through the Gmail connector.
Step 2
Check
Each value is tested against a plausible range; anything not found is marked, not guessed.
Step 3
Interpret
Outliers, trends and target gaps computed; reasons named in the mails carried into the summary.
Step 4
Track & send
Measures stay on the board with owner and due date until closed; after confirmation, board and weekly slide go to the distribution list.
03

What it changes

Before
·About an hour of manual data transfer per week
·Status reporting fills the first half of the meeting
·Reasons retold from memory, measures in the minutes
After
→A short review of what was recognised
→Outliers and trends already separated, each with a proposed next step
→Context from the mails in the summary; every measure with owner, due date and its effect on the KPI, week after week until closed

Limitation. The public demo shows the board on synthetic data. Reading the mails and sending the weekly slide belong to the workflow around it and run in a private environment, since they touch a real mailbox.

Built with Claude. One HTML file, no backend. Outliers, trend and target comparison are computed rather than generated; the model reads the mails and phrases the summary, and the page says which part is which.

Personal app

Workout generator

Five questions — equipment, level, focus, time, and a free field for injuries or anything else to consider — produce a session built for exactly that situation. It then runs step by step with a timer, from warm-up to cool-down. Takes about thirty seconds to try.

Where this came from

My own week. Training slots are short and turn up unannounced, and the deciding what to do was reliably eating the slot.

Generate a workout Live version with Claude The live version needs a Claude account; the button on the left always works
Situation
Short, unplanned slots; the deciding eats the time
What it does
Five inputs in, a personalised guided session out
Effect
Training starts immediately and fits the minutes available
01

The problem

  • Equipment, focus and whatever currently hurts change from week to week, so a written plan goes stale
  • Choosing exercises, order and duration takes ten minutes of a thirty-minute window
  • Asking a chatbot produces something plausible that uses equipment which isn't there
  • And the session described as thirty minutes is over in twenty-four
02

How it works

Step 1
Five questions
Equipment, level, focus, time — plus a free field for injuries or a low-energy day.
Step 2
Session built
Only the equipment selected, the exact minutes available, every exercise explained.
Step 3
Guided run
Warm-up, work, rest and cool-down, one screen at a time with a timer.
03

What it changes

Before
·Ten minutes of deciding before anything starts
·Plans that ignore the knee and the kettlebell
·Sessions that overrun or end early
After
→Thirty seconds of input, then training
→Only the equipment at hand, injuries taken into account
→The time available, used exactly, guided by a timer

Built with Claude. One HTML file, no backend. The timing is calculated rather than requested, the answer is checked against the selection before it is shown, and if Claude is unavailable a built-in exercise library produces the session instead. Not medical advice.
